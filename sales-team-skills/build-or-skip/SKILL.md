# Build or skip

AI amplifies whatever state your content and process are already in. Point an agent at clean, agreed-upon inputs and it speeds things up. Point it at contradictory playbooks and stale enablement decks and it produces confident nonsense faster than a human ever could.

So the first question for any build request isn't "can we build it?" It almost always can. The question is whether the conditions exist for it to work after the demo.

## When to use

- A leader or rep brings a build idea
- Prioritizing a backlog of requests
- Deciding whether to keep investing in something already shipped

## The four gates

Run every idea through these in order. Stop at the first hard fail.

**1. Is there a source of truth?**
Can you point to where the right answer lives? Messaging, ICP, qualification criteria, pricing rules. If the answer is "it depends who you ask," the build will encode one person's opinion as policy.

**2. Does the team agree what good looks like?**
Get two managers to grade the same three outputs. If they disagree, you can't evaluate the tool, and you can't prove it helped.

**3. Will the inputs hold still?**
If the playbook changes every quarter, the build needs an owner and a refresh path from day one, or it rots. Factor that cost in now.

**4. Does it change a behavior, or just a format?**
Reformatting CRM data into a nicer summary is fine. Changing what a rep does before a call is better. Know which one you're buying.

## Verdicts

Give exactly one:

- **Build.** Gates pass. Name the smallest version that could be in a rep's hands this week.
- **Fix the content first.** The pain is real but a gate failed. Name the specific doc, field or decision that has to exist, and who owns it. This is often the highest-value answer and the least popular one.
- **Configure, don't build.** Something the team already pays for does 80% of it. Say which feature.
- **Skip.** Low frequency, low variance, or the real problem is a people or incentive issue. Say that plainly.

## Kill criteria

For every "build," write down up front what would make you stop:

- Usage drops below X reps per week after the second iteration
- Output needs heavy rework more than Y% of the time
- The owner of a source input leaves and nobody picks it up

Agreeing to kill criteria before building is what makes "be clear about what isn't worth building" a practice instead of a mood.

## Output

A one-page decision note: the request in the requester's words, gate results, verdict, smallest viable version or the prerequisite, kill criteria, and who said yes.
