# Review bottleneck mapper

Review is where content goes to wait. A draft that took three days to write spends three weeks bouncing between a product manager who's in planning, a legal reviewer who gets everything regardless of risk, and a VP who rewrites the opening paragraph in their own voice. Nobody's doing anything wrong on purpose. The process just has no shape.

This skill maps how review actually works, finds where the time goes, and redesigns it.

## What you need from the user

- The reviewers for a typical piece: roles, not names.
- The order they review in, if there is one.
- How long review takes, roughly, end to end. If they have dates from a few recent pieces (draft submitted, each approval, published), that's ideal.
- What reviewers actually change. Ask for an example of a heavily marked-up draft if they have one.
- Content types that go through different paths (a blog versus a white paper versus a press release).

## How to run it

1. **Map the chain.** For each reviewer, capture: role, what they're supposed to check, what they actually change, typical turnaround, and whether they block publishing.
2. **Find the waits.** Separate working time from waiting time. In most teams, more than 80% of review time is a draft sitting in someone's queue.
3. **Diagnose each stall.** Common causes:
   - **No scope**: reviewers don't know what they're checking, so they check everything.
   - **Serial when it could be parallel**: reviewers wait for each other with no need to.
   - **Wrong-sized review**: a low-risk blog gets the same legal review as a pricing page.
   - **Rewriting, not reviewing**: senior reviewers editing for taste.
   - **No deadline**: no expectation of turnaround, so review waits behind everything else.
   - **Late reviewers**: a stakeholder who sees the piece for the first time at final review and wants to change the angle.
4. **Redesign.** Produce the new process.

## Output format

**Current chain**

| Reviewer | Supposed to check | Actually changes | Typical wait | Blocks publish? |
|---|---|---|---|---|

**Where the time goes**: a short read on working versus waiting time, and the single biggest stall.

**Redesigned process**:
- **Review tiers**: two or three tiers by risk (for example, a light tier for blogs and social, a standard tier for gated assets, and a full tier for anything with pricing, security, legal or competitive claims). Who reviews at each tier.
- **Reviewer scopes**: one line per reviewer on what they check and what they leave alone. "Legal checks claims, comparisons and trademark use. Legal doesn't edit tone."
- **Review windows**: turnaround per tier, and what happens when a window is missed (for example, silence after the window counts as approval for light-tier content).
- **Early alignment**: where angle and messaging get agreed before drafting, so the final review isn't where the direction changes.
- **Escalation**: who breaks ties when reviewers disagree.

**Messages to send**: a short note to reviewers introducing their scope and window, written to sound collaborative rather than like a policy memo.

## Rules

- Don't propose removing a reviewer who carries real risk (legal, security, regulated claims). Narrow their scope instead.
- "Silence counts as approval" only belongs on low-risk tiers. Say so explicitly.
- If the bottleneck is one specific senior person, say that plainly but kindly, and design around it (early alignment, a delegate, or a smaller review scope).
- Base the redesign on what the user told you. If you don't know a reviewer's turnaround, mark it as unknown rather than guessing.
